## Introduction

At this part of the course, we are concerned about what is the Gospel in the context of the early church. We have already seen how the early Christians understood their mission in the Hellenistic culture through the writings of the Apostolic Fathers. Now, we will explore the challenges that the early church faced in defining and defending the Gospel message, particularly in the context of Gnosticism.

(gnosticism)=
In a context of polytheism, dualism, syncretism, and mystery religions, the early Christians had to articulate what they believed about Jesus Christ and his significance for salvation. Gnosticism, with its emphasis on secret knowledge (gnosis) and its dualistic worldview, posed a significant challenge to the early church's understanding of the Gospel. Therefore, the church needs a story, a narrative that makes sense of who Jesus is, what he has done, and what it means for humanity. That is made through the creation of the creeds.

## A Word About *Sola Scriptura*

*Sola Scriptura* is a theological doctrine that asserts that the Bible is the sole infallible source of authority for Christian faith and practice. This doctrine emerged during the 20th century, known as the "Scripture Alone" principle, and is based on the works from the Protestant Reformation in the 16th century as a response to certain practices and teachings of the Roman Catholic Church, which reformers believed were not grounded in Scripture.

It is very relevant to reflect on the fact that Scripture Alone can be very misleading and even harmful if not properly understood. The early church did not have a New Testament canon as we have today. The canon was formed over several centuries, and the early Christians relied on oral tradition, apostolic teachings, and various writings that were considered authoritative at the time. Therefore, the early church had to rely on creeds, councils, and the guidance of the Holy Spirit to discern the true teachings of Christianity.

If we are to take *Sola Scriptura* seriously, we need to understand that the Bible itself points us to the community of faith, the church, as the context in which Scripture is interpreted and understood. The early church's creeds and councils were not attempts to add to Scripture but rather to clarify and articulate the essential truths of the Christian faith in response to various challenges and heresies.

Therefore, we must rely on more than Scripture alone. We must also rely on:

- Creed (the story, the narrative that makes sense of Scripture)
- Canon (the authoritative books that make up Scripture)
- Holy Spirit (the guide and teacher of the church)
- Leaders (the teachers and pastors who help interpret and apply Scripture)

If any one of these is missing or misunderstood, our understanding of the Gospel can be distorted. That could be seen in the medieval church, where the leaders abused their authority, leading to corruption and the need for the Reformation. It could also be seen in some modern churches that reject creeds and traditions, leading to a fragmented and individualistic approach to faith.

## Gnosticism

Gnosticism was a diverse and complex religious movement that emerged in the early centuries of Christianity. It was characterized by its emphasis on secret knowledge (gnosis) as the key to salvation and its dualistic worldview, which posited a stark contrast between the spiritual and material realms.

:::{figure} ../figures/gnosticism-cover.png
A traveller peers through an opening in the firmament in this illustration from Camille Flammarion's L'atmosphère : météorologie populaire (Paris: Hachette, 1888), p. 163
:::

(demiurge)=
Gnostics believed that the material world was created by a lesser deity, often referred to as the *Demiurge*, who was distinct from the true, transcendent God. They viewed the material world as inherently evil or corrupt, while the spiritual realm was seen as pure and good. According to Gnostic beliefs, human beings contained a divine spark or spirit that was trapped within their physical bodies, and salvation involved awakening this divine spark through gnosis.

Gnosticism presented a significant challenge to the early Christian church, as it offered an alternative understanding of Jesus Christ and his mission. Gnostics often portrayed Jesus as a divine being who imparted secret knowledge to a select few, rather than as the incarnate Son of God who came to redeem humanity through his death and resurrection. This led to various Gnostic sects and teachings that diverged from orthodox Christian beliefs, prompting the early church to respond with creeds and councils to clarify the true nature of Christ and the Gospel message.

Therefore, we can define Gnosticism by the following characteristics:

| Characteristic | Description |
|----------------|-------------|
|metaphysical dualism | Belief in two opposing realms: the spiritual (good) and the material (evil) |
| material world is wrong/meaningless/evil | The physical world is created by a lesser deity (Demiurge) and is inherently flawed |
| escape/“salvation” through esoteric knowledge | Salvation is achieved by acquiring secret knowledge (gnosis) that awakens the divine spark within |
| knowledge is given only to “insiders” | Gnosis is accessible only to a select group of individuals who are initiated into the Gnostic teachings |
| sense of powerlessness, therefore elaborate myths | Gnostic myths often involve complex cosmologies and narratives that explain the origin of the world and humanity's plight |
| syncretistic | Gnosticism often incorporated elements from various religious traditions, including Christianity, Judaism, and paganism |
| ascetics or libertines | Gnostic groups varied in their ethical teachings, with some advocating for strict asceticism while others embraced libertine practices |

From now onwards we will study the many different types of Gnosticism that emerged in the early centuries of Christianity, including Marcionism and Docetism, and how the early church responded to these challenges in order to defend and articulate the true Gospel message.

## Gnostic Movements

Some of the major Gnostic movements that existed in the early centuries of Christianity include:

| Movement | Founder | Years of Existence | Description |
|----------|---------|--------------------|-------------|
| Marcionism | Marcion of Sinope | c. 85–160 CE | Emphasized a radical dualism between the God of the Old Testament and the God of the New Testament; rejected the Old Testament and created a unique canon of Christian texts. |
| Valentinianism | Valentinus | c. 100–160 CE | Developed a complex cosmology involving divine emanations (Aeons) and the concept of the Pleroma; emphasized the role of gnosis for salvation. |
| Docetism | Various Gnostic teachers | 1st–3rd centuries CE | Asserted that Jesus Christ only appeared to have a physical body and did not truly possess one; denied the full humanity of Jesus. |
| Sethianism | Various Gnostic teachers | 2nd–4th centuries CE | Centered around the figure of Seth, the third son of Adam and Eve; developed elaborate myths about the creation of the world and the role of divine beings. |
| Ophitism | Various Gnostic teachers | 2nd–4th centuries CE | Focused on the serpent (Ophis) as a symbol of wisdom and liberation; viewed the serpent in the Garden of Eden as a positive figure who brought knowledge to humanity. |
| Manicheanism | Mani | c. 216–276 CE | Founded by the prophet Mani; combined elements of Gnosticism, Christianity, Zoroastrianism, and Buddhism; emphasized a cosmic struggle between light and darkness. |

These movements, among others, contributed to the rich and diverse landscape of Gnostic thought in the early Christian era. Each movement had its own unique beliefs, practices, and interpretations of Christian teachings, which often diverged significantly from orthodox Christianity. The early church responded to these challenges by developing [creeds](#creeds) and doctrines that affirmed the true nature of Christ and the Gospel message, ultimately shaping the course of Christian theology and practice.

### Valentinianism

(valentinianism)=
Valentinianism was one of the major Gnostic Christian movements in the second century, founded by Valentinus (c. 100–160 CE), a prominent Gnostic teacher. Valentinus taught a complex cosmology that involved a series of divine emanations (Aeons) from a supreme, unknowable God. According to Valentinian belief, the material world was created by a lesser deity, often identified with the God of the Old Testament, who was ignorant of the higher spiritual realities.

Valentinians believed that human beings contained a divine spark or seed that originated from the higher spiritual realm. This divine spark was trapped within the material body, and salvation involved awakening this spark through gnosis (knowledge) of one's true spiritual nature and origin. Valentinian teachings emphasized the role of Jesus Christ as a revealer of this secret knowledge, who came to impart gnosis to those capable of receiving it.

Valentinianism also had a distinctive view of the church and sacraments. Valentinians often practiced a form of initiation that involved baptism and a special rite known as the "bridal chamber," which symbolized the union of the soul with its divine origin.

:::{figure} ../figures/Flavia_Sophe_inscription.jpg
Valentinian inscription from the grave stele of Flavia Sophe, referencing the bridal chamber. [Source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentinianism)
:::

**The Pleroma**

(pleroma)=
Central to [Valentinian](#valentinianism) cosmology is the concept of the *Pleroma*, the fullness of the divine realm inhabited by the Aeons. The Pleroma is a complex hierarchy of divine beings, each representing different aspects of the divine nature. At the top of this hierarchy is the ultimate God, from whom all Aeons emanate in pairs, reflecting a balance of masculine and feminine principles.

(sophia)=
The fall of Sophia, one of the Aeons, led to the creation of the material world and the Demiurge, who is responsible for the flawed physical universe. The divine spark within humans is seen as a fragment of the Pleroma that has become trapped in the material world, and salvation involves returning this spark to its original divine source through gnosis.

:::{figure} ../figures/SophiaMystical.png
This image is an illustration of the Gnostic figure Sophia, and it is a plate from the book Geheime Figuren der Rosenkreuzer (Secret Figures of the Rosicrucians) published in Altona, Germany, in 1785. [Source: Wikimedia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7c/SophiaMystical.jpg/330px-SophiaMystical.jpg)
:::

The life outside the [Pleroma](#pleroma) is characterized by ignorance and suffering, as individuals are unaware of their true spiritual nature and are trapped in the material world. Three substances are identified in Valentinian thought:

| Substance | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Hylic (matter) | The material world and physical body, associated with ignorance and corruption |
| Psychic (soul) | The soul or psyche, capable of receiving gnosis and salvation |
| Pneumatic (spirit) | The divine spark within humans, originating from the Pleroma |

[Irenaeus of Lyons](#irenaeus), in his work *Against Heresies*, provides a distorted and ironic account of Valentinian cosmology:

> "There exists a power which I call a *Gourd*. And along with this *Gourd* there exists a power which I call *Utter-Emptiness*. This *Gourd* and *Emptiness*, since they are one, produced a fruit, everywhere visible, eatable, and delicious, which fruit-language calls a *Cucumber*. Along with this *Cucumber* exists a power of the same essence, which I call a *Melon*. These powers—the *Gourd*, *Utter-Emptiness*, the *Cucumber*, and the *Melon*—brought forth the remaining multitude of the delirious melons of Valentinus." (Against Heresies 1.11.4)

### Docetism

(docetism)=
Docetism was a prominent Gnostic belief that emerged in the early centuries of Christianity, asserting that Jesus Christ only *appeared* to have a physical body and did not truly possess one. The term "docetism" is derived from the Greek word "dokein," which means "to seem" or "to appear."

According to docetic teachings, Jesus was a purely spiritual being who merely took on the appearance of a human form, but his physical body was an illusion. This belief was rooted in the Gnostic worldview, which viewed the material world as inherently evil or corrupt, and therefore, it was inconceivable for a divine being to take on a physical body.

Docetism posed a significant challenge to orthodox Christian beliefs, particularly regarding the incarnation and the nature of Jesus Christ. The early church firmly rejected docetic teachings, emphasizing the full humanity and divinity of Jesus Christ as essential to the Christian faith. The [Council of Nicaea](#nicaea) in 325 CE and subsequent councils affirmed the orthodox understanding of Christ's incarnation, rejecting docetism and other heresies that denied the true humanity of Jesus.

It is important to note that docetism is best understood as a collection of widespread tendencies rather than a movement originating from one person.

:::

### Marcionism

(marcionism)=
Marcionism was a significant Gnostic Christian movement founded by Marcion of Sinope (c. 85–160 CE). Marcion was a wealthy shipowner who became a prominent figure in the early Christian church. He is best known for his radical theological views, which led to his excommunication from the orthodox Christian community. He was the son of a bishop in Sinope, a city on the southern coast of the Black Sea (modern-day Turkey).

Marcion's teachings were characterized by a strong dualism, which posited a distinction between the God of the Old Testament and the God of the New Testament. He believed that the God of the Old Testament was a lesser, vengeful deity (the [Demiurge](#demiurge)) who created the material world, while the true God revealed in Jesus Christ was a higher, benevolent being who offered salvation through grace. This radical reinterpretation of Scripture led Marcion to reject the Old Testament entirely and to create his own canon of Christian texts, which included an edited version of the Gospel of Luke and ten of Paul's epistles.

Marcionism posed a significant challenge to the early church, as it forced Christian leaders to clarify their understanding of the relationship between the Old and New Testaments and to defend the unity of God's revelation in Scripture. The movement ultimately led to the development of the orthodox Christian canon and the affirmation of the Old Testament as an integral part of the Christian faith.

:::{figure} ../figures/Apostle_John_and_Marcion_of_Sinope,_from_JPM_LIbrary_MS_748,_11th_c.jpg
Apostle John and Marcion of Sinope, from JPM Library MS 748, 11th century. [Source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcionism#)
:::

:::{admonition} Marcion's Community
:class: simple
After being accused of heresy and excommunicated from the orthodox Christian church around 144 CE, Marcion went on to establish his own church, which spread throughout the Roman Empire. Marcionism gained a significant following, particularly among Gentile Christians who were attracted to Marcion's emphasis on grace and his rejection of Jewish law.
:::

## Gnosticism Tendencies Nowadays

### Doctrine as Salvific Knowledge

"We are not brains on a stick that will enable our entry to the Pleroma."

Christians should avoid a salvation-by-knowledge mentality. That is, avoid the mentality that salvation is about acquiring the right knowledge or correct beliefs in order to be saved.

### Ignoring Tradition

Avoid the mentality that we can read the Bible without the guidance of tradition. Sola scriptura is misleading, a recipe for heresy. The Bible is our only infallible authority, but it is not our only authority. Without the guidance of tradition to read Scripture, we could be Gnostic Christians, Jehovah Witnesses, Mormons, etc.

### Dualistic Jesus
Avoid "Star Trek Jesus," a savior who beams our souls up out of this world. "Star Trek Jesus" has nothing to do with daily human existence.

### Material World is Evil

Avoid a worldview that confines God to a spiritual realm that is disconnected from the rest of creation. This is a spiritualizing tendency—the cultural inclination to imagine that one can talk meaningfully about theology apart from any substantive reflection on politics, economics, or culture; acting as if theology is somehow independent of or sealed off from these more mundane realities.

### A contemporary example

In his famous "Letter from a Birmingham Jail," Martin Luther King Jr. critiques the white moderate for being more devoted to "order" than to justice, for preferring a negative peace which is the absence of tension to a positive peace which is the presence of justice. This attitude reflects a dualistic separation between the sacred and the secular, where social issues are dismissed as irrelevant to the gospel. King writes:

> "I must confess that over the past few years I have been gravely disappointed with the white moderate. I have almost reached the regrettable conclusion that the Negro’s great stumbling block in his stride toward freedom is not the White Citizen’s Counciler or the Ku Klux Klanner, but the white moderate … In the midst of blatant injustices inflicted upon the Negro, I have watched white churchmen stand on the sideline and mouth pious irrelevancies and sanctimonious trivialities. In the midst of a mighty struggle to rid our nation of racial and economic injustice, I have heard many ministers say: ‘Those are social issues, with which the gospel has no real concern.’ And I have watched many churches commit themselves to a completely other worldly religion which makes a strange, un-Biblical distinction between body and soul, between the sacred and the secular."
