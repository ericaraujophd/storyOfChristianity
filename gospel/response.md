The [Marcion](#marcionism) attempt to put together a [canon](#canon) of Christian Scripture forced the early church to respond. Still, the canon took several centuries to be formalized. The early church also needed to respond to the Gnostic challenge by formulating creeds that articulated the essential beliefs of Christianity. Finally, the church had to ensure the continuity of apostolic teaching through a system of apostolic succession.

The following responses helped the early church to clarify and defend the true Gospel message against Gnostic distortions.

## The Canon

(canon)=
The canon of Scripture refers to the collection of books that are recognized as authoritative and inspired by God within the Christian tradition. The process of canonization was gradual and involved discernment by early church leaders and councils. The canon served as a safeguard against heretical teachings and provided a foundation for Christian doctrine.

The practical needs for a canon arose from several factors:

1. need for a set text for worship and teaching
2. need for devotional reading material
3. need for a source of theological reflection
4. need for a set text to translate into other languages

### The Canon around 100 A.D.

At 100 A.D., there was no fixed canon of Christian Scripture. The "New Testament" books were not yet collected and defined as "Scripture". Still, early Christian writers (e.g., Polycarp and Ignatius) quote from the Gospels and Paul's letters, as well as from other Christian writings and oral sources. Paul's letters were collected late in the first century. Matthew, Mark and Luke were brought together by AD 150.

That is to say that by the mid-2nd century, there was a core collection of Christian writings that were widely recognized and used in the church. However, there were still debates about which books should be included in the canon.

### The Muratorian Canon around 200 A.D.

The Muratorian Canon is the earliest known list of New Testament books, dating to around 200 A.D. It includes most of the books that are now part of the New Testament, but it also excludes some books that are now considered canonical (e.g., Hebrews, 1 and 2 Peter, 3 John) and includes some books that are now considered apocryphal (e.g., The Shepherd of Hermas, The Revelation of Peter).

By 250 A.D., there was a more widely accepted canon of Christian Scripture, which included the four Gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, the Pauline Epistles, and some other writings. However, there were still debates about certain books, such as Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, Jude, The Shepherd of Hermas, and Letter to Barnabas.

By 300 A.D., there were still a dispute over books like James, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John and Jude. Some books were excluded such as The Shepherd of Hermas and The Revelation of Peter.

That is to say that by the early 4th century, there was a more or less settled canon of Christian Scripture, but there were still some debates and variations in different regions.

### The Council of Carthage in 397 A.D.

(carthage)=
The Council of Carthage in 397 A.D. is often cited as a significant moment in the formal recognition of the New Testament canon. The council produced a list of canonical books that closely resembles the modern New Testament. This list included the 27 books that are now universally recognized as canonical.

The apparent criteria for canonicity included:

1. Apostolic authorship or connection
2. Orthodox content consistent with Christian teaching
3. Antiquity of the text
4. Usage in liturgical practice by the church

The books listed in the Council of Carthage's canon are:

- The four Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John)
- The Acts of the Apostles
- The Pauline Epistles (Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, 1 and 2 Thessalonians, 1 and 2 Timothy, Titus, Philemon)
- The General Epistles (Hebrews, James, 1 and 2 Peter, 1, 2, and 3 John, Jude)
- The Book of Revelation

The definition of the [canon](#canon) at the Council of Carthage helped to solidify the New Testament as we know it today. However, it is important to note that the canon was not universally accepted immediately, and some regional variations persisted for some time. The process of canonization was gradual and involved discernment by church leaders and councils over several centuries.

Another question that would come up later is the question of how to interpret the canon. And that's the direction will turn next, as we look at the life and works of Origen of Alexandria.

## The Creeds

(creeds)=
The creeds are concise statements of Christian belief that summarize the essential doctrines of the faith. They serve as a "rule of faith" that helps to define orthodox Christian beliefs and distinguish them from heretical teachings, such as those found in Gnosticism. The most well-known creeds are the [Apostles' Creed](#apostles-creed) and the [Nicene Creed](#nicene-creed). These creeds affirm key beliefs about the nature of God, Jesus Christ, the Holy Spirit, and the church.

The main purposes of the creeds are to:

1. organize teaching for catechumens
2. express identity at baptism
3. unify the church in all times and places
4. express communal faith in worship
5. guide reading of Scripture
6. fight heresy

Quite often the church in a particular place created their own creedal formula, mostly similar to other churches in the neighboring cities. The [Apostles' Creed](#apostles-creed) is an example of a creed that likely developed from the baptismal confession used in the early church. This creed was probably created in Rome, around the year 150 A.D., and was called "R" by scholars. This creed at the time was also known as the "symbol of faith". The word symbol in that context refereed to "a means of recognition", like a badge or a flag. The creed served as a way for Christians to identify themselves and their shared beliefs.

### 📜 The Apostles' Creed

(apostles-creed)=
The Apostles' Creed is one of the oldest and most widely used creeds in Christianity. It is traditionally attributed to the apostles, although it was likely developed over time by early Christian communities. The creed affirms essential Christian doctrines, including the nature of God, the incarnation, crucifixion, resurrection, and ascension of Jesus Christ, as well as beliefs about the Holy Spirit, the church, and eternal life.

### 📜 The Nicene Creed

(nicene-creed)=
The Nicene Creed was formulated at the [First Council of Nicaea](#nicaea) in 325 AD and later expanded at the [First Council of Constantinople](#constantinople) in 381 AD. It was created to address various heresies, particularly Arianism, which denied the full divinity of Jesus Christ. The Nicene Creed affirms the belief in the Trinity and the full divinity and humanity of Jesus Christ.

### Three ways to frame the creeds to our contemporary churches

Some contemporary churches struggle with the idea of creeds, seeing them as outdated or irrelevant. Here are three ways to frame the creeds to help congregations appreciate their significance:

:::{pull-quote}
Jesus is the most amazing, confounding, alarming, and captivating person ever to walk the planet. Even as the early church loved and worshiped Jesus, they found describing him beyond available words. Over time they assembled carefully formed truths about Jesus into creeds.  Creeds were one way to clearly describe and confess their faith in this most amazing person ever to live, and a way to invite novices into deeper understanding about who he is. We invite you to say a creed now as a way to enter into the mystery of Jesus.
:::

## Apostolic Succession

Apostolic succession is the belief that the authority and teachings of the apostles have been passed down through an unbroken line of bishops. This concept was crucial for maintaining the integrity of Christian doctrine and practice. The early church emphasized the importance of apostolic succession to ensure that the teachings of Jesus and the apostles were preserved and transmitted faithfully to future generations.

## The Work of the Holy Spirit in Montanism


(montanism)=
Montanism was a charismatic movement that emerged in the late 2nd century AD, founded by Montanus, a Christian prophet from Phrygia (modern-day Turkey). Montanism emphasized the ongoing work of the Holy Spirit in the life of the church, particularly through prophecy and ecstatic experiences. Montanus, along with two prophetesses, Priscilla and Maximilla, claimed to receive direct revelations from the Holy Spirit, which they believed were a continuation of the apostolic age.

The two main characteristics of Montanism were its emphasis on new prophecy and its rigorous moral standards. Montanists believed that the Holy Spirit was actively guiding the church through new revelations, which sometimes challenged established church teachings and practices. They also advocated for a strict moral code, including asceticism, fasting, and celibacy.

Among the main montanist figures were:

- **Montanus**: The founder of the movement, who claimed to be a prophet filled with the Holy Spirit.
- **Priscilla and Maximilla**: Two prophetesses who played significant roles in the Montanist movement, also claiming to receive divine revelations.
- [**Tertullian**](#tertullian): An early Christian writer and theologian who later became a Montanist. He defended Montanism and its emphasis on the Holy Spirit in his writings.
- **Epiphanius of Salamis**: A church father who wrote against Montanism, documenting its beliefs and practices in his work "Panarion."
- **Apollonius of Ephesus**: An early Christian writer who also opposed Montanism and provided valuable historical accounts of the movement.
