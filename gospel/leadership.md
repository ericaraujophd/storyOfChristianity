The fourth aspect of the response to Gnosticism is the establishment of leadership structures within the early Christian communities. As the church grew and faced various challenges, it became necessary to have clear leadership to maintain doctrinal integrity and unity. The early church developed a hierarchical structure with bishops, presbyters (elders), and deacons.

Even though the roles were still developing, bishops were seen as the successors of the apostles and held the highest authority in the local church. They were responsible for teaching, governing, and safeguarding the faith. Presbyters assisted the bishops in pastoral care and administration, while deacons served in practical roles, such as caring for the poor and managing church resources.

These offices were not distinguished until Tertullian (ca. 160-225 A.D.) wrote about them in his work "On the Prescription of Heretics." The development of this leadership structure helped to ensure that the teachings of the apostles were preserved and transmitted faithfully, providing stability and continuity for the early Christian communities.

## Cultural Influences on Leadership

There were mainly three cultural influences on the development of church leadership:

1. **Social Influence**: The "household of God" model was influenced by the Roman household structure, where the paterfamilias (head of the household) held authority over family members. This model was adapted to the church context, with bishops serving as spiritual heads of the Christian community.
2. **Philosophical Influence**: school leadership structures, such as those in Stoicism and Platonism, also influenced the church's leadership model. These schools often had a hierarchical structure with a head teacher and assistants, which provided a framework for organizing church leadership.
3. **Jewish Influence**: The early Christian church inherited many leadership concepts from Jewish synagogue structures, where elders and teachers played significant roles in guiding the community.

### 1. Social Influence

Everyday Greco-Roman households consisted of approximately 50 persons from at least three generations, in addition to which there would be servants, slaves, tenants, clients, and others.

The household **servants** were called *diakonoi* (deacons). They were to obey the head of the household as they would obey God.

The **head servant** was called *episkopos* (bishop). He was in charge of the other servants and represented the master. Servants were to obey the head servant as if he was the master.

So, by analogy, a person in the church should obey the bishop/*episkopos* (the head servant) as one would obey God. A bishop is to be humble as a slave but he also speaks and acts on God’s behalf. He serves the members of the household, administering funds and meeting spiritual needs.

### 2. Philosophical Influence

Many schools of philosophy co-existed with the Early Christianity in the Greco-Roman world. Regardless, Christianity claimed to be the true philosophy, and the church was God’s school.

:::{admonition} Justin Martyr's School
:class: tip
Justin Martyr (c. 100–165 AD) was an early Christian apologist and philosopher who established a Christian school in Rome.
:::

Greco-Roman philosophical schools were communities intent on **spiritual formation**: A teacher and his disciples dialogued, ate together, and sometimes lived together. The goal of philosophical discourse was to **persuade people to live in a certain way and to practice it**.

Philosophy was not simply a way to think about life but a way of instilling attitudes and disciplined living; **a disciple's thinking and affections and actions needed to be converted**. So disciples engaged in an inner battle between the old and the new life.

Christianity looked similar to these philosophical schools.

:::{admonition} Leadership Terms from Philosophy
:class: tip
"Disciples" was Justin’s favorite term for Christians; he never speaks of *episkopoi* or *presbyteroi*. He describes the leader of the eucharist as a 'presider' (proestōs)—a term often used for the organizational head of philosophical schools.
:::

### 3. Jewish Influence

In [The Apostolic Constitutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_Constitutions), OT Israelite structures have parallels in the church. Three church offices were shaped by the duties of the Israelites.  

- ➡️ The bishop is somewhat similar to the high priest,
  - ➡️ presbyters to priests,
    - ➡️ and deacons to Levites.

There also seemed to be a correlation between synagogue roles and developing church leadership roles.

The administrator of the church, an *episkopos*, acquired some functions of a Jewish *archisynagogos* ('ruler/head/administrator of the synagogue'). 

The *diakonoi* in the churches acquired some functions of the almoners and attendants in the Jewish synagogues.

Moreover, Christians, who, like Jews abstained from many normal civic functions, may have begun to organize themselves as a kind of distinct 'ethnic community,' which was neither Jew nor Gentile but claimed a 'heavenly citizenship.' The 'seniors' of this community were a kind of governing council which had the authority to appoint and advise the *episkopos*.

## Pre-Christedom Views on Leadership

These are five key points about leadership in the early church before Christendom:

1. Priesthood of all believers.
2. Ministry which met community needs determined "offices".
3. Practice, not merely a position.
4. Appointment to church office did not confer spiritual authority, but it recognized spiritual authority that was already evident.
5. Current theory for the first two centuries re. the "three offices":
   - multiplicity of functions (readers, singers, porters, deaconesses, widows, virgins, orphans);
   - bishop dominant;
   - deacons perhaps more prominent ‘office’ than presbyters.

| Leadership Views | Meaning |
|------------------|---------|
| 1. Priesthood of all believers | Every believer is a leader. Part of one’s baptism identity is being anointed as a priest who will serve the High Priest Jesus Christ. |
| 2. Ministry which met community needs determined "offices" | As people exercised their gifts to meet needs, some of these ministries were communally recognized as official roles, e.g., prophets, healers, exorcists, widows, readers, virgins, subdeacons, hosts of agape meals, porters, grave diggers (fossores), ... |
| 3. A practice, not merely a position | Ministry leadership, like the Christian life, was seen as a practice. E.g., Confessors: their faith and practice gave them the highest spiritual authority in their communities, not an "office" (position) as modern Christians think of "office." |
| 4. Appointment to church office did not confer spiritual authority, but it recognized spiritual authority that was already evident. | People who are exerting their gifts and have their spiritual authority are regocnized by receiving an office, and not the contrary. |

Regarding point 5, in the first two centuries, we do not see a distinction between the roles of "clergy" and "laity."  [Tertullian](#tertullian) (early 3c) is the first Western theologian to make this distinction.

There was probably not a clear threefold ministry (bishop, elder, deacon) as we conceive it today: modern attempts to trace the development of offices in the church have often been "the investigator’s own denominational face at the bottom of a deep well" (Young 157).

In Acts and the Pastoral Epistles "elders" (*presbyteroi*) appears, but it is unclear what their relationship with an "overseer" or "bishop" (*episkopos*) is. They could be alternative terms for the same subject.

The multiplicity of functions (readers, singers, porters, deaconesses, widows, virgins, orphans) and the dominance of the bishop are more noticeable than a threefold office ([The Apostolic Constitutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_Constitutions)).

:::{admonition} *episkopos* and *diakonoi*
:class: important
Bishops and deacons may have been the only appointed 'officers' of a congregation. The church was 'God’s household' (1 Tim 3:15): the *episkopos* was 'God’s steward' (Titus 1:7) and the *diakonoi* were the servants.  
:::

(presbyter)=
:::{admonition} "presbyter"
:class: important
An ambiguous term that suggested seniority: these senior citizens carried the community memory and therefore were responsible to teach the apostolic tradition. The *episkopos* turns to the senior members for advice, so they acted as an advisory council.
:::
