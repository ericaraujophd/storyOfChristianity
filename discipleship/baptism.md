The catechumenate process culminated in baptism, which was not only a rite of initiation but also a profound act of discipleship. Baptism symbolized the believer's identification with the death, burial, and resurrection of Jesus Christ (Romans 6:3-4). It marked the beginning of a new life in Christ and a commitment to follow Him.

Baptism was typically performed by immersion in water, signifying the washing away of sin and the believer's new life in Christ. The act of immersion itself was a powerful symbol of dying to the old self and being raised to new life (Colossians 2:12).

In this class we will explore the significance of baptism in the early church and its role in forming disciples. We will also discuss how baptism served as a public declaration of faith and a means of grace for the believer.

## House-Churches and Baptism

The most common place to perform baptisms in the early church were the house-churches. These were private homes where Christians gathered for worship, fellowship, and instruction. House-churches provided a more intimate and communal setting for baptism, allowing new believers to be welcomed into the Christian community.

That is to say that ideally baptism should be performed in a place where there is plenty of water, as many examples from the Bible, including Jesus' baptism in the Jordan River (Matthew 3:13-17).

The house at Duras-Europos was built shortly after 200 A.D. and adapted to be used as a Christian meeting place. One room was enlarged to hold around 100 people, with a small platform for the bishop, and in the corner a baptismal font.

## Baptism and the Gospel

Baptism in the early church was not merely a ritual but a profound proclamation of the gospel. It symbolized the believer's union with Christ in His death and resurrection, signifying the forgiveness of sins and the gift of new life (Acts 2:38; Romans 6:4).

Some of the significant theological themes associated with baptism in the early church include:

| Theme | Significance | Scriptural References |
|-------|--------------|----------------------|
| Belonging to Christ | United to Him in His death and resurrection. | Romans 6:3; Colossians 2:12; Mark 10:38 |
| Belonging to the Church | Incorporated into the body of Christ. | 1 Corinthians 12:13; Galatians 3:26-28 |
| The Holy Spirit | Receiving the gift of the Spirit. | Acts 2:38; 1 Corinthians 6:11 |
| Forgiveness of Sins | Cleansing from sin and new life. | Acts 2:38; Acts 22:16; 1 Peter 3:21; Hebrews 10:22 |
| New Creation/Life | Becoming a new creation in Christ. | John 3:5; Titus 3:5 |

The experience of baptism is also connected to the concept of **anemnesis**, or remembrance, where the believer recalls and participates in the saving events of Christ's life, death, and resurrection. Through baptism, believers are reminded of their identity in Christ and their call to live as His disciples in the world.

(anemnesis)=
:::{admonition} Anemnesis
:class: tip
Anemnesis is a Greek term meaning "remembrance" or "memorial." In Christian liturgy, it refers to the act of recalling and proclaiming the significant events of salvation history, particularly the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ. Anemnesis serves to connect believers with the foundational events of their faith, fostering a sense of continuity and participation in the ongoing story of God's redemptive work.
:::

## The Process of Christian Initiation

The process of Christian initiation in the early church typically involved several key steps:

1. **Catechumenate**: As discussed in the previous lecture, this was a period of instruction and preparation for new converts.
2. **Pre-baptismal Rites**: Before baptism, catechumens often underwent rites of exorcism to renounce Satan and his works. They were also anointed with oil as a sign of being set apart for God.
3. **Baptism**: The actual act of baptism, usually performed by immersion in water, symbolizing the washing away of sin and the new life in Christ.
4. **Chrismation**: After baptism, the new believer was anointed with holy oil (chrism), symbolizing the gift of the Holy Spirit.
5. **Eucharist**: The newly baptized would then participate in the Lord's Supper, signifying their full inclusion in the Christian community.
6. **Instruction and Discipleship**: Following baptism, new believers would continue to receive instruction and be nurtured in their faith as they grew in discipleship. This step was also known as **mystagogy**.

### Pre-baptismal ceremonies

Before the actual baptism, several important ceremonies and rituals were typically observed to prepare the catechumens for this significant step in their faith journey:

**1 - Renunciation of Sin**

The catechumens publicly renounced their former way of life, including any ties to paganism or sin, as a preparation for their new life in Christ. This part of the ceremony would also involve exorcisms to symbolically free the catechumens from the influence of evil spirits.
**2 - Led to the Baptismal Font**

The catechumens were ceremonially led to the baptismal font, often accompanied by prayers and hymns, symbolizing their journey towards new life.

- Mark 15:22 mentions Jesus being led to Golgotha, which can be seen as a parallel to the catechumens being led to baptism. 
- Mark 15:46 states that Jesus was laid in a tomb after his crucifixion, symbolizing the death to sin that baptism represents.

**3 - Removal of Clothing**

Catechumens would remove their clothing before baptism, symbolizing the shedding of their old self and the beginning of a new life in Christ.

- Gn 2:25 states that Adam and his wife were both naked and felt no shame, symbolizing the innocence and purity of the newly baptized.
- Col 3:9-10 speaks of putting off the old self and putting on the new self, which aligns with the symbolism of removing clothing before baptism.
- Ephesians 4:22-24 also emphasizes this transformation, urging believers to put off their old self and be renewed in the spirit of their minds.
- John 19:23-25 describes the soldiers dividing Jesus' garments and casting lots for his clothing, which can be seen as a foreshadowing of the believer's new identity in Christ.

**4 - Anointing with Oil**

Catechumens were often anointed with oil before baptism, symbolizing their consecration to God and the presence of the Holy Spirit.

- Luke 10:34 describes the Good Samaritan anointing the wounds of the injured man, symbolizing healing and restoration.
- James 5:14 encourages the anointing of the sick with oil in the name of the Lord, symbolizing spiritual healing and empowerment.
- Mark 6:13 mentions the apostles anointing the sick with oil and healing them, symbolizing the power of the Holy Spirit at work.
- Hebrews 1:9 speaks of God's anointing of the Son with the oil of gladness, symbolizing joy and divine favor.
- Psalm 23:4-5 describes God anointing the psalmist's head with oil, symbolizing blessing and abundance.
- Psalm 45:7 speaks of God's anointing of the king with the oil of gladness above his companions, symbolizing honor and divine approval.
- Romans 11:24 mentions being grafted in by faith, which can be symbolized by the anointing with oil as a sign of inclusion in God's people.

:::{admonition} Anointing with Oil
:class: tip
For Cyril of Jerusalem, anointing is a symbol of the share that the candidate will have in the richness of Christ the true olive tree.
:::

Other symbols related to anointing with oil:

- protection against evil (e.g. Psalm 23:4-5)
- preparation for combat with the devil: candidates are rubbed with oil like an athlete, Christ’s athlete
- ownership, like the branding of a sheep

These pre-baptismal ceremonies emphasized the transformative nature of baptism and the commitment required to follow Christ.

## The Baptism

The actual act of baptism, usually performed by immersion in water, symbolizing the washing away of sin and the new life in Christ. But not any water! The early church preferred "living water," such as rivers, streams, or springs, which symbolized the life-giving and cleansing power of God. Also, luke warm water was avoided, as it could symbolize spiritual lukewarmness (Revelation 3:16).

:::{admonition} Baptism according to the Didache
:class: tip
"Now about baptism: this is how to baptize. Give public instruction on all these points, and then immerse in running water, 'in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.' If you do not have running water, immerse in some other. If you cannot in cold, then in warm. If you have neither, then pour water on the head three times 'in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.' Before the baptism, the one who baptizes and the one being baptized must fast, and any others who can. And you must tell the one being baptized to fast one or two days beforehand" (Didache 7:1-4).
:::

The reason for running water can be found in many Scripture passages that highlight the symbolism of water in relation to spiritual cleansing and new life:

- Genesis 1:2 - "Now the earth was formless and empty, darkness was over the surface of the deep, and the Spirit of God was hovering over the waters." This passage emphasizes the creative power of God associated with water.
- Exodus 14:21-22 - "Then Moses stretched out his hand over the sea, and all that night the Lord drove the sea back with a strong east wind and turned it into dry land. The waters were divided, and the Israelites went through the sea on dry ground, with a wall of water on their right and on their left." This event symbolizes deliverance and new beginnings through water.
- Matthew 3:16 - "As soon as Jesus was baptized, he went up out of the water. At that moment heaven was opened, and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and alighting on him." This passage highlights the significance of baptism in Jesus' life and ministry.
- Mark 1:10 - "Just as Jesus was coming up out of the water, he saw heaven being torn open and the Spirit descending on him like a dove." This passage reinforces the connection between baptism and the presence of the Holy Spirit.
- Luke 3:21-22 - "When all the people were being baptized, Jesus was baptized too. And as he was praying, heaven was opened and the Holy Spirit descended on him in bodily form like a dove. And a voice came from heaven: 'You are my Son, whom I love; with you I am well pleased.'" This passage emphasizes the divine affirmation of Jesus' identity through baptism.
- John 5:3 - "In these lay a great number of disabled people—blind, lame, and paralyzed—waiting for the moving of the waters." This passage highlights the healing power associated with water.
- John 7:38 - "Whoever believes in me, as Scripture has said, rivers of living water will flow from within them." This passage emphasizes the life-giving nature of water in relation to faith in Christ.
- Revelation 22:1 - "Then the angel showed me the river of the water of life, as clear as crystal, flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb." This passage symbolizes eternal life and divine provision through water.

## Architectural Elements Related to Baptism

Early Christian architecture often incorporated specific elements related to baptism, reflecting its significance in the life of the church. Some of these elements include:

| Element | Description | Significance |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| Baptismal Font | A basin or pool used for baptism, often located near the entrance of the church. | Symbolizes the entry into the Christian life and the cleansing of sin. |
| Narthex | The entrance area of the church, where catechumens would gather before baptism. | Represents the transition from the outside world into the sacred space of the church. |
| Baptistery | A separate building or designated area within the church complex specifically for baptisms. | Emphasizes the importance of baptism as a distinct and significant rite of initiation. |
| Font Shape | Often circular or octagonal in shape. | The circular shape symbolizes eternity and the infinite nature of God, while the octagonal shape represents new beginnings and resurrection. |
| Steps Leading to the Font | Steps leading down into the baptismal font. | Symbolizes the descent into death to sin and the ascent into new life in Christ. |
| Steps Leading from the Font | Steps leading up from the baptismal font. | Symbolizes the new life and resurrection in Christ after baptism. |
| The Good Shepherd | Often depicted in baptismal scenes, representing Christ's care and guidance. | Symbolizes the nurturing aspect of the Christian faith and the call to follow Christ. |

:::{admonition} Baptismal Font Shapes
:class: tip

- Circular fonts symbolize eternity and the infinite nature of God.
- Cruciform fonts represent the death and resurrection of Christ.
- Rectangular fonts can symbolize a tomb, where the baptized person is buried with Christ and rises to new life.
- Octagonal fonts represent Jesus being resurrected on the eighth day.
- Hexagonal fonts can symbolize the six days of creation, with the seventh day representing rest and the new creation in Christ or the day when Jesus died.
:::

## Post-Baptismal Practices

After baptism, several practices are often observed to help the newly baptized integrate into the Christian community and grow in their faith. These practices may include:

1. **Chrismation**: The newly baptized is anointed with holy oil (chrism) as a sign of the gift of the Holy Spirit and their incorporation into the body of Christ.
2. **Clothing in White Garments**: The newly baptized may be clothed in white garments, symbolizing their new purity and righteousness in Christ.
3. **Spiritual Sealing**: The newly baptized may receive a spiritual seal, often in the form of a cross marked on their forehead or hand, symbolizing their identity as followers of Christ.
4. **First Communion**: The newly baptized may receive their first communion, participating fully in the Eucharist and the life of the church.

The table below summarizes these post-baptismal practices:

| Practice | Description | Significance | Scriptural References |
|----------|-------------|--------------|----------------------|
| Chrismation | Anointing with holy oil (chrism) | Sign of the gift of the Holy Spirit and incorporation into the body of Christ | 1 Sam. 10:1; 16:1-13; 2 Kings 9:6; Luke 4:16; Acts 4:27; Acts 10:38; Matt. 3:16; Mk 1:10; Lk 3:22; Ex. 19:6; 1 Peter 2:5, 9; Rev. 1:6; 5:10 |
| Clothing in White Garments | The newly baptized is clothed in white garments | Symbolizes new purity and righteousness in Christ | Mark 14:51-52; Mark 16:5; Gal. 3:27; 2 Cor. 5:1-5; Rev. 7:9-14 |
| Spiritual Sealing | A spiritual seal is marked on the forehead or hand | Symbolizes identity as followers of Christ | Eph. 1:13-14; 2 Cor. 1:21-22 |
| First Communion | The newly baptized receives their first communion | Participation in the Eucharist and life of the church | John 6:53-58; 1 Cor. 11:23-26 |

Cyril of Jerusalem annointed the newly baptized with chrism on various parts of the body, symbolizing their participation in the life of Christ:

- On the forehead: to lose the shame of Adam (Exodus 28:36-38; Ezekiel 9:4; Revelation 13:16-17)
- On the ears: to hear the word of God (Isaiah 35:5; Isaiah 50:5; Matthew 11:15; Mark 7:33)
- On the nose: to smell the fragrance of Christ (2 Corinthians 2:14-16; Ephesians 5:2)
- On the chest: to be armed against the devil (Isaiah 59:17; Ephesians 6:14; 1Timothy 5:8)

The newly baptized are then welcomed into the Christian community, exchanging the "kiss of peace" with other believers (Romans 16:16; 1 Corinthians 16:20; 2 Corinthians 13:12; 1 Thessalonians 5:26; 1 Peter 5:14).

They are then invited to participate in the Eucharist, symbolizing their full inclusion in the body of Christ (1 Corinthians 10:16-17; 11:23-26). In their first communion, they receive a compound of honey and milk together with the wine and bread, symbolizing the sweetness and nourishment of the Christian life, as well as the entrance in the promised land (Psalm 19:10; Proverbs 24:13-14; Isaiah 7:15; 1 Peter 2:2).

As they leave the service, they are incited to give alms to the poor, symbolizing their new commitment to love and serve others as followers of Christ (Matthew 25:35-40; Luke 3:11; Acts 20:35; James 1:27).

## Separation Between Baptism and Holy Supper

In the Early Church, baptism and the Lord’s Supper were united. Baptism and the post-baptismal anointing (later referred to as confirmation) led directly to the Table. E.g., Cyprian and Augustine practiced infant baptism AND infant communion.

The Eastern Church has always maintained the unity of baptism and the Eucharist. In the Western Church, however, a separation developed over time. By the Middle Ages, it became common for baptized infants to wait until they were older (often around age seven) to receive their first communion.

The practice of infant communion continued in the West until the 13th century, but several medieval developments gradually ended the practice.

Some of the reasons for the change in the Western Church include:

1. Strengthening the power of bishops: In the West, only a bishop was allowed to confirm a baptism. Since a bishop was not available in some areas for many years, infant participation in Communion was sometimes delayed.

:::{admonition} Medieval mentality
:class: tip
Power to the hierarchy!
:::

Some bishops would pass by some villages for years without visiting baptizing from the horseback as passing by.

2. The doctrine of transubstantiation (13th cent.): The substance of bread and wine changes into the actual body and blood of Jesus.

:::{admonition} Medieval mentality
:class: tip
Don’t let children drop Jesus’ actual body on the floor or slobber into his real blood!
:::

3. Heavy penitential piety: Kids can’t do serious penance like adults can.  

:::{admonition} Medieval mentality
:class: tip
Kids shouldn’t come to the Eucharist until their penance makes them worthy.
:::

4. Making up a doctrine: confirmation. In this so-called sacrament, individuals confirm their place in the church.

:::{admonition} Medieval mentality
:class: tip
Confirmation is a sacrament even though its biblical support is underwhelming.
:::

5. Parents who thought confirmation wasn’t a sacrament were punished: Parents and their kids were banned from communion if the kids were not confirmed.

:::{admonition} Medieval mentality
:class: tip
Confirmation is a sacrament and people who disobey the hierarchy must be punished.
:::

In the 16th century, Reformers clarified that confirmation is not a sacrament, and renamed it "public profession of faith."

1 Corinthians 11 is the proof text, but John Calvin’s new interpretation downplays the literary and historical context and misinterprets "body" in 11:27.

> "We offer poison [...] to our tender children" at the Supper, for it is vain to expect them "to distinguish rightly the holiness of Christ’s body."

The Reformed tradition inherited a MEDIEVAL, not a Reformed, practice.
