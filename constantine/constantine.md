(constantine)=
Constantine the Great (c. 272-337 A.D.) was a Roman Emperor who played a crucial role in the history of Christianity. His conversion to Christianity and subsequent policies had a profound impact on the Christian church and its relationship with the Roman Empire. He got converted to Christianity in 312 A.D. after the Battle of the Milvian Bridge, where he reportedly saw a vision of the Christian cross and heard the words "In this sign, conquer."

(edict_of_milan)=
After his conversion, Constantine issued the Edict of Milan in 313 A.D., which granted religious tolerance to Christians and allowed them to practice their faith openly without fear of persecution. This marked a significant turning point for Christianity, as it transitioned from a persecuted minority religion to a favored and eventually dominant religion within the Roman Empire.

In 380 A.D., Emperor Theodosius I declared Christianity the official state religion of the Roman Empire with the Edict of Thessalonica. This further solidified the position of Christianity within the empire and led to the suppression of pagan practices.

## From Early to Imperial Christianity

The conversion of Constantine and the subsequent adoption of Christianity as the state religion had several significant effects on the Christian church:

- Christians moved from the margins to becoming the religion of the state. From margins to the centre of culture.
- Christians moved from a weak and persecuted minority to a powerful institution with political influence. From poor and humble places of worship to grand basilicas funded by the empire.
- Christians moved from illegal gatherings in homes and catacombs to public worship and state-sponsored events. From secret meetings to public processions and ceremonies.
- Christians moved from [paroikoi](#paroikoi) (sojourners) to citizens of the empire with legal rights and privileges. From outsiders to insiders.

## Constantine's Legacy

Constantine's conversion and policies laid the foundation for the Christian church's growth and influence in the centuries that followed. The church became intertwined with the state, leading to both opportunities and challenges for Christianity. While the church gained political power and resources, it also faced the risk of becoming too closely aligned with secular authorities.

His motives for converting to Christianity are still debated among historians. Some argue that he genuinely embraced the faith, while others suggest that his conversion was a strategic move to unify the empire under a single religion. Regardless of his motives, Constantine's impact on the Christian church and its history is undeniable.

Among the reactions from Christians to Constantine's conversion and the subsequent changes in the church, some embraced the new status and opportunities, while others expressed concern about the potential compromises and challenges that came with being aligned with imperial power. Five postures from early Christians regarding Constantine's conversion are:

1. **Grateful and uncritical**: Constantine ended gladiatorial games, punished sexual crimes more harshly, improved the lives of women, slaves and children, returned confiscated property, built churches, oulawed inhumane treatment of animals, and supported the poor. Many Christians were simply grateful for these benefits and did not question his motives or the implications of his conversion.
2. **Fleeing to remote places**: Some Christians, particularly monks and ascetics, chose to withdraw from society and seek solitude in remote areas. They were concerned about the potential corruption and compromises that could arise from the church's close association with imperial power.
3. **Break away from the majority church**: Some Christians, such as the [Donatists](#donatists) in North Africa, rejected the authority of the mainstream church that was now aligned with the empire. They believed that the church had compromised its purity and integrity by accepting imperial support.
4. **Pagan reactions**: Some pagans viewed Constantine's conversion as a betrayal of traditional Roman values and religious practices. They saw Christianity as a threat to the established order and resisted its growing influence. One example is Emperor Julian the Apostate (r. 361-363 A.D.), who attempted to revive paganism and reduce the influence of Christianity during his brief reign.
5. **Critical but engaged**: Some Christians, such as [Arius](#arius) and [Athanasius](#athanasius), engaged in theological debates and controversies regarding the nature of Christ and the relationship between the Father and the Son. They were critical of certain aspects of the church's alignment with imperial power but remained actively involved in shaping Christian doctrine and practice.

Regarding the leaders who were critical but engaged with Constantine's conversion, Lesslie Newbigin will state that:

> Much has been written about the harm done to the cause of the gospel when Constantine accepted baptism, and it is not difficult to expand on this theme. But **could any other choice have been made?** When the ancient classical world [...] ran out of spiritual fuel and turned to the church as the one society that could hold a disintegrating world together, should the church have refused the appeal and washed its hands of responsibility for the political order? [...]
>
> It could not do so if it was to be faithful to its origins in Israel and the ministry of Jesus. It is easy to see with hindsight how quickly the church fell into the temptations of worldly power. It is easy to point [...] to the glaring contradictions between the Jesus of the Gospels and his followers occupying the seats of power and wealth. And yet we have to ask, would God’s purpose as it is revealed in Scripture have been better served, if the church had refused all political responsibility, if there had never been a "Christian" Europe, if all the churches for the past two thousand years had lived as persecuted minorities [...]? I find it hard to think so.
