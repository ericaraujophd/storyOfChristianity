In the Greek culture, the leaders are the philosophers. In the Roman culture, the leaders are the politicians and military leaders. In the early church, the leader is Jesus Christ.

As 4th century Christian leaders theologically articulated the nature of leadership, they were intentionally transforming Greco-Roman [paideia](#paideia), a powerfully influential tradition in ancient culture. [Paideia](#paideia) is the process of forming ideal citizens in a wise way of life. This formation process had been developed over 1,000 years and involved training in rhetoric, philosophy, mentoring, moral modeling, etc.

By adapting Greco-Roman [paideia](#paideia) into a Christian process of formation, Christian leaders transformed their culture as rapidly and profoundly as any period of Christian history.

This transformation was accelerated by Emperor Constantine's conversion to Christianity and the subsequent adoption of Christianity as the favored religion of the Roman Empire. This shift in political power had significant implications for church leadership and organization. We will explore three ways in which the [paideia](#paideia) of church leadership was transformed by church leaders at the time.

## 1. The Divine Philosopher is the only Leader

Not Homer or Plato. Not even a Christian bishop. There is only one true Teacher, the Divine Philosopher, Jesus Christ.  All human leaders are servants and imitators of Christ the Divine Philosopher.

Augustine tells his listeners:

> We stand in this elevated position as your instructors, but we are also your fellow-students in this school under our one Teacher.

And:

> I feed you on what I am fed on myself. I am just a servant; I am not the head of the house. I set food before you from the pantry that I too live on, from the Lord’s storerooms, from the banquet of that householder who 'for our sakes became poor, though he was rich, in order to enrich us from his poverty' (2 Cor. 8:9).

## 2. Goal of leadership

The goal of leadership is to form a thriving human being.

On the surface, this goal is similar to Greco-Roman [paideia](#paideia), but the definition of a thriving human being is quite different. 

The leadership theology of the early church could be summarized as follows:

- Glory of God ➜ thriving human being
- Thriving human being ➜ person in communion with God. i.e., the end is in the beginning: union/communion with God is the original purpose for which we were created and the end goal of salvation.
- A person in communion with God ➜ a person conformed to Jesus
- A person conformed to Jesus ➜ a person who loves God and neighbor

Therefore, a leader's role is **psychagogy**, that is, guiding souls to union with God (i.e., toward greater love).

(psychagogy)=
In Greco-Roman [paideia](#paideia), philosophical schools were engaged in psychagogy, the guidance of souls into a wise way of life. So early Christian leaders argued that the school of the truest philosophy (the church) was also engaged in psychagogy, in a soul therapy which was guiding people into maturity in Christ which is the wisest way of life.

:::{admonition} The Apostle Paul was a model of Christian psychagogy
:class: tip
Each phrase of his letters was calibrated precisely to the needs of the souls he was guiding. The early church read Paul as a pastor and preacher and consummate spiritual director, not a systematic theologian. Early church pastors sought to imitate Paul by guiding people toward faith, hope and love.
:::

Early Christian bishops saw themselves first and foremost as **guides of souls**, not as administrative officials, not as resident systematic theologians, not as religiously-oriented psychologists. Above all, pastoral leaders were **spiritual guides** in the process of being conformed to the image of Christ.

Of course, being a spiritual guide got you involved in a lot of human affairs: deciding disputes, supporting the needy (e.g., challenging exploiters in times of famine), showing hospitality to strangers, standing up to abusive emperors and others whose moral example was witnessed by the public.

[Psychagogy](#psychagogy), the process of guiding souls to conform to Christ, is an extremely difficult task. We humans do not conform easily to becoming better lovers of God and others, because our loves are confused and disordered.

Gregory of Nazianzus says that "The leadership of men and women, the craftiest and most complicated of all creatures, seems to me the art of arts and the science of sciences."

The primary means to guide people to greater union with God included:

1. Character modeling
2. Communal practices:  prayer, fasting, eucharist, charity, etc.
3. Teaching

Each of these means was used in Greco-Roman [paideia](#paideia), but Christians transformed their content and methods.

:::{admonition} Ephesians 4
Ephesians 4 as an example of what a leader should do: equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ.
:::
 
## 3. Character as the Basis of Leadership

Character provides a spiritual authority which is the basis of everything leaders do, because character:

- Attracts souls
- Persuades souls
- Teaches souls

### Character Attracts Souls

Charater is seen as magnet, *gravitas*.  A person of character draws others to them. People want to be around them. They want to listen to them. They want to learn from them.

**Ambrose:** "Who seeks for a spring in the mud; no one quenches their thirst with dirty water?"

### Character Persuades Souls

We persuade by who we are more than by what we know.

**John Chrysostom:** "We lead by persuasion, not force."

### Character Teaches Souls

A visible object lesson. An icon. Palpable holiness.

**Augustine:** a bad example can kill people, a good example is life-giving.

**Ambrose** once rejected a person for ministry because he thought the person walked too arrogantly.

## Virtues and Vices of Leaders

- Typical vices of leaders are: pride, anger, greed, lust, sloth, envy, gluttony.
- Critical virtues of leaders are: love, humility, courage, temperance, justice, prudence, faith, hope.

## Prominent Leaders from 4th to 5th Century

The 4th and 5th centuries were a remarkable period of leadership in the early Christian church. This era saw the emergence of some of the most influential theologians, bishops, and church fathers who played pivotal roles in shaping Christian doctrine, defending the faith against heresies, and guiding the church through times of change and challenge.
