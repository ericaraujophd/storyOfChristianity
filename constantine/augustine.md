(augustine)=
Augustine of Hippo (354-430 A.D.) was one of the most influential theologians and philosophers in Christian history. Born in Thagaste (modern-day Algeria), Augustine's early life was marked by a search for truth, which eventually led him to embrace Christianity. His conversion experience in 386 A.D., as recounted in his autobiographical work *Confessions*, is one of the most famous spiritual journeys in Christian literature.

His main conversion event took place in a garden in Milan, where he heard a child's voice chanting "take and read." Interpreting this as a divine command, he opened the Bible to Romans 13:13-14, which led to his profound spiritual transformation.

Augustine was a manichaeist for almost a decade before converting to Christianity under the influence of his mother, Monica, and the preaching of [Ambrose of Milan](#ambrose). After his conversion, Augustine was baptized by [Ambrose](#ambrose) in 387 A.D. He later returned to North Africa, where he was ordained a priest in 391 A.D. and became the Bishop of Hippo in 395 A.D.

Augustine's theological contributions are vast and varied. He is perhaps best known for his works on original sin, grace, and the nature of the church. His writings, including *The City of God* and *On Christian Doctrine*, have had a lasting impact on Christian theology and Western philosophy.

Augustine faced mainly three significant heresies during his lifetime: Manichaeism, Donatism, and Pelagianism. He wrote extensively against these heresies, defending orthodox Christian doctrine and shaping the church's understanding of key theological concepts.

### Augustine against Manichaeism

(manichaeism)=
Augustine's early involvement with Manichaeism, a dualistic religion that posited a cosmic struggle between good and evil, deeply influenced his later theological reflections. After his conversion to Christianity, Augustine wrote *Confessions*, where he detailed his journey away from Manichaeism and towards Christian faith. He critiqued Manichaean beliefs, particularly their views on the nature of evil and the material world, arguing instead for a Christian understanding of creation and the problem of sin.

For Augustine, evil was not a substance or force in opposition to good, as the Manichaeans claimed, but rather a privation of good—a corruption of what is inherently good. This perspective laid the groundwork for his later theological explorations on sin and grace. According to Augustine, human will is weakened by original sin, and only through God's grace can individuals be restored to righteousness. He used the concept of disordered love to explain how humans often misdirect their affections towards lesser goods, leading to sin.

In summary, evil is temporary and its origin is in the **human will**.

### Augustine against Donatism

(donatism)=
Donatism was a schism that arose in North Africa, centered around the issue of the sanctity of the church and its ministers. Donatists argued that clergy who had lapsed in faith during persecution could not validly administer sacraments. Augustine vehemently opposed this view, asserting that the efficacy of the sacraments did not depend on the moral purity of the minister but on the grace of God.

Augustine's arguments against Donatism emphasized the unity of the church and the importance of forgiveness and reconciliation. He contended that the church is a mixed body of saints and sinners, and that God's grace operates through the sacraments regardless of the personal holiness of the clergy. Augustine's efforts were instrumental in suppressing the Donatist movement and reaffirming the catholic understanding of the church.

In summary, church looks more like 'wheat and tares' instead of 'pure bride', and sacraments are given by Christ, not the priest.

### Augustine against Pelagianism

(pelagianism)=
Pelagianism, named after the British monk Pelagius, was a theological doctrine that denied the concept of original sin and asserted that human beings could achieve salvation through their own efforts without the need for divine grace. Augustine strongly opposed Pelagianism, arguing that it undermined the necessity of God's grace for salvation. He maintained that all humans inherit original sin from Adam, which corrupts human nature and renders individuals incapable of choosing good without God's intervention. Augustine's writings against Pelagianism, particularly in his work *On the Grace of Christ and Original Sin*, were crucial in shaping the church's doctrine on grace and human nature.

Pelagianism starting point was the belief in the inherent goodness of human nature and the capacity for moral perfection through free will. Augustine countered this by emphasizing the total depravity of human nature due to original sin and the indispensable role of divine grace in enabling humans to turn towards God and achieve salvation. Augustine was also concerned that Pelagianism led people to be disheartened when they failed to live up to moral standards, as it placed the burden of salvation entirely on human effort.

In summary, sin is not the primary result of bad habits and social conditioning which people can overcome and grow to full adulthood. The original sin and the corruption make us as infants who are dependent on God's grace.
