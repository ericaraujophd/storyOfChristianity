In this section, we will explore the lives and contributions of Athanasius of Alexandria and the Cappadocian Fathers, who were instrumental in defending and developing the Nicene faith during the 4th century. Part of the context of their work was the [Arian controversy](#arianism), which challenged the orthodox understanding of the nature of Christ and the Trinity.

---

## Athanasius of Alexandria (d. 373)

> The results of the incarnation of the Savior are such and so many, that anyone attempting to enumerate them should be compared to a person looking upon the vastness of the sea and trying to count its waves. — Athanasius

Icon of Saint Athanasios, Antivouniotissa Museum, Kerkyra [Source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athanasius_of_Alexandria)

(athanasius)=
Athanasius was the Bishop of Alexandria and one of the most important theologians of the early Christian church. He is best known for his staunch defense of the Nicene Creed against Arianism, which denied the full divinity of Jesus Christ.

Athanasius was a young man when he attended the [Council of Nicaea](#nicaea) in 325 AD as a deacon. He was so dark and short that his enemies called him "the black dwarf". He became bishop of Alexandria in 328 AD and served in that role for over four decades, during which he faced multiple exiles due to his opposition to Arianism.

His origin is unknown, but he was likely born in Alexandria, Egypt and was possibly a Coptic. He would constantly be in contact with the monastic communities in the desert around Alexandria, which influenced his spirituality and theology. They would later offer him refuge during his exiles.

@gonzalez2010story [pg. 200] will attribute Athanasius' theological brilliance to his close relationship with the desert monks and his monastic discipline, his roots among the people of Alexandria, and his deep study of Scripture.

Athanasius' most famous work is "On the Incarnation," in which he articulates the orthodox understanding of the incarnation of Christ. He argued that Jesus Christ is both fully God and fully human, and that through his incarnation, death, and resurrection, humanity is offered salvation and reconciliation with God. This work is even prior to his controversy with the Arians [@gonzalez2010story].

(arianism)=
Arius taught that Jesus, the Son of God, was not truly God, but a lesser being, a creature. According to Arius, there was a time when the Son did not exist, and he was created by God the Father. This view challenged the orthodox understanding of the Trinity, which holds that the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are co-eternal and of the same substance (homoousios).

As a result of the Arianism controversy, many monks left the cities to keep a pure faith in Jesus incarnate God.

Alexander, bishop of Alexandria, wanted Athanasius to be his successor. When Alexander died in 328, Athanasius was elected bishop. But before his death, Athanasius had withdrawn to the desert in order to keep his quiet life. There was no escape. As bishop, Athanasius continued to oppose Arianism and defend the Nicene faith. He faced significant opposition from Arian sympathizers and was exiled multiple times by various Roman emperors who supported Arianism. In the same year he became bishop, Constantine revoked his sentence exiling Arius.

(eusebius-of-nicomedia)=
Eusebius of Nicomedia, an Arian bishop, convinced Constantine to exile Athanasius under some lies. He was exiled to Trier in 335 AD. He would return to Alexandria in 337 after Constantine's death.

Eusebius of Nicomedia would baptize Constantine on his deathbed in 337 AD, which further solidified Arian influence in the empire. His sons, Constantius II, Constantius, and Constans, would allow exiled bishops to return to their churches.

Athanasius was exiled again after a certain Gregory, a rival, convinced the governor that Athanasius was not legitimate bishop. As he tried to steal the churches from Athanasius, the situation escalated, and Athanasius saw himself forced to flee again to the desert. He would smuggle himself to Rome in 339 AD, where he was welcomed by Pope Julius I.

His time in Rome was fruitful, as he was able to gather support for the Nicene faith. Pope Julius I convened a synod that affirmed Athanasius' position as the legitimate bishop of Alexandria and condemned Arianism and treated Gregory as an usurper.

After the death of Constantine II, the two other brothers agreed to allow Athanasius to return to Alexandria. He was received with great joy by the people. For ten years he would lead the church in Alexandria, strengthening the Nicene faith and combating Arianism.

Still, there was a problem: Emperor Constantius was an Arian sympathizer. He would endures Athanasius' presence in Alexandria due to his brother's insistence. He needed his brother's support against the usurper Magnentius. In 353 A.D., Constantius felt strong enough to exile Athanasius again. This time, Athanasius fled to the desert monks, who hid him for five years and would transfer him from one monastery to another whenever officers of the empire would approach Athanasius' hideout.

After Constantius' unexpected death, Athanasius was able to return to Alexandria once more. The new emeperor, Julian, was not interested in Christian affairs and wanted to restore paganism. Athanasius would remain in Alexandria until his death in 373 AD.

### Key Accomplishments Despite Persecution

**Defended Nicene Orthodoxy**: Spent entire 45-year episcopate opposing Arianism and defending homoousios (Christ's full divinity)

**Survived 5 Exiles Totaling 17+ Years**: Endured more exiles than any major church figure of his era

**Wrote Seminal Theological Works**: 

- On the Incarnation (c. 318-335)
- Four Orations Against the Arians (356-362)
- Apology Against the Arians
- Apology to Constantius
- History of the Arians

**Wrote Life of Saint Anthony** (c. 360): Pioneering biography that promoted monastic life throughout Western Christianity

**First to Formally Define New Testament Canon** (367): His Festal Letter of 367 first formally lists all 27 books of NT that are canonized today

**Built Monastic Networks**: Developed strong relationships with desert monks (especially Pachomius) who supported him during exiles and spread his teachings

**Earned 'Athanasius Contra Mundum'**: Became symbol of unwavering faith—'Athanasius against the world'—for standing alone against imperial and ecclesiastical pressure

### Imperial Support & Opposition Timeline

- **Constantine I (306-337)**: Initially supportive; exiles Athanasius due to Eusebian influence; death restores him
- **Constantine II (337-340)**: Supportive (East)
- **Constans (337-350)**: Strongly supportive (West); his protection crucial during second exile
- **Constantius II (337-361)**: Implacably opposed; pro-Arian; orders three of five exiles; most dangerous opponent
- **Julian the Apostate (361-363)**: Pagan; paradoxically allows return but later briefly exiles him
- **Jovian (363-364)**: Pro-Nicene; brief reign allows respite
- **Valens (364-378)**: Pro-Arian; orders fifth exile but soon reverses decision
- **Valentinian I (364-375)**: Western emperor; maintains pro-Nicene stance

## Cappadocian Fathers (4c)

(cappadocian-fathers)=
To talk about the Capaddocian Fathers we need to start with a woman. Macrina the Younger was the sister of Basil the Great and Gregory of Nyssa. She was an ascetic and spiritual teacher who had a profound influence on her brothers and the development of monasticism in the Eastern Church. Later, Gregory of Nazianzus would also join this group of theologians that defended the Nicene faith and developed the doctrine of the Trinity.

### Macrina the Younger (or the Teacher)

(macrina)=
Macrina was born around 324 AD in Caesarea, Cappadocia (modern-day Turkey). She came from a devout Christian family and was known for her piety and ascetic lifestyle. After the death of her parents, Macrina chose to live a life of celibacy and devotion to God. She established a monastic community for women, which became a model for Christian monasticism in the East.

:::{figure} figures/Saint-Makrina-the-Younger-ascetic-teacher-sister-of-Saint-Basil-Gregory-Peter-Icon-Orthodox-abbess-great-Monastery-1.jpg
Icon of Saint Macrina the Younger
:::

Macrina had a great influence on the lives of her brothers. Basil the Great, after receiving his education in Constantinople and Athens, returned home and was a bostful and prideful man. Macrina confronted him about his arrogance and led him to embrace a life of humility and service. She also influenced Gregory of Nyssa, who would later write about her, highlighting her virtues and spiritual wisdom.

In her deathbed conversation with Gregory of Nyssa, Macrina articulated a profound understanding of the resurrection and the Christian hope in eternal life. Her teachings and example had a lasting impact on her brothers and the broader Christian community. This conversation is recorded in Gregory's work ["On the Soul and the Resurrection."](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf205.x.iii.ii.html)

### Gregory of Nyssa

(gregory-of-nyssa)=
Gregory of Nyssa was born around 335 AD in Caesarea, Cappadocia. He was the younger brother of Basil the Great and a close friend of Gregory of Nazianzus. Gregory had a very different personality from his brother Basil. He was more contemplative and mystical in his approach to theology.

He married a woman named Theosebia, who was also deeply involved in the Christian faith. After her death, Gregory decided to pursue a monastic life and was ordained a bishop. He became the bishop of Nyssa, a small town in Cappadocia. His writings reflect his life style, focusing on the mystical and spiritual aspects of the Christian faith.

He became a bishop ordained by his brother Basil. After the death of Valens and Basil, he became one of the leading defenders of Nicene orthodoxy. He participated in the [Council of Constantinople](#council-of-constantinople) in 381 AD, abd was requested to travel throughout the Empire by Theodosius I to combat Arianism.

### Basil the Great

(basil)=
Basil the Great was born around 329 AD in Caesarea, Cappadocia. He came from a wealthy and influential Christian family. Basil received a classical education in Constantinople and Athens, where he studied rhetoric, philosophy, and theology. After completing his studies, Basil returned to Cappadocia. @gonzalez2010story [pg. 210] notes that Basil was "puffed up with his own wisdom" as he soon got a position teaching rhetoric in Caesarea. When his brother Naucratius passed unexpectedly, Basil was deeply affected and began to reconsider his life. Influenced by his sister Macrina, he decided to resign and started his journey learning the "secrets of religious life" and to live a monastic life.

[Gregory of Nazianzus](#gregory-of-nazianzus) was Basil's close friend from their studies in Athens. They shared a deep commitment to the Christian faith. They also founded a community of monks for men close to Annesi, in the same model as Macrina's community for women.

Basil was later ordained a presbyter against his will. After some conflict with the bishop of Caesarea, Basil returned to his monastic community. Later, when Arianism resurged in Cappadocia, Basil was invited to join forces with the bishop of Caesarea to defend the Nicene faith.

In his return to Caesarea, Basil faced a dramatic situation. The crops were destroyed by the bad weather, and rich people were hoarding food to sell it at high prices. Basil organized the distribution of food to the poor and established a system of charity in the outskirts of the city. This system included a hospital, an almshouse, and a hospice for travelers. This complex was known as the Basiliad.

In one of his writings ("Homily on Luke's Works"), Basil express his views on social justice and the responsibility of Christians to care for the poor:

> If one who takes the clothing off another is called a thief, why give any other name to one who can clothe the naked and refuses to do so? The bread that you withhold belongs to the poor; the cape that you hide in your chest belongs to the naked; the shoes rotting in your house belong to those who must go unshod.

Basil was elected the bishop of Caesarea in 370 AD. After his election, the Arian emperor Valens prepared a visit to Caesarea, intending to overthrow Basil. However, Basil's strong leadership and the support of the people prevented Valens from carrying out his plan. After being threatened to have his goods confiscated, exile, torture, or death, Basil boldly confronted Valens:

> All that I have that you can confiscate are these rags and a few books. Nor can you exile me, for wherever you send me, I shall be God's guest. As to torture you should know that my body is already dead in Christ. And death would be a great boon to me, leading me sooner to God.

He passed away a few months before the [Council of Constantinople](#concil-of-constantinople) in 381 AD, which would further affirm the Nicene faith and condemn Arianism.

### Gregory of Nazianzus

(gregory-of-nazianzus)=
Gregory of Nazianzus was born around 329 AD in Arianzus, near Nazianzus, Cappadocia. He came from a devout Christian family and was a close friend of Basil the Great. Gregory received a classical education in Constantinople and Athens, where he studied rhetoric and philosophy. After completing his studies, Gregory returned to Cappadocia and became a teacher of rhetoric in Nazianzus.

Gregory was ordained a presbyter against his will. He tried to avoid getting leadership positions as he wanted to pursue a contemplative life. To his dismay, later Basil made him bishop of Sasima, a small town in Cappadocia. This act caused a rift between the two friends, as Gregory felt that Basil had forced him into a position he did not want. They never reconciled.

In 379 AD, Gregory was called to Constantinople. In 380 AD, the new Emperor Theodosius I, a Nicene Christian, ordained Gregory to become the Archbishop of Constantinople. Gregory reluctantly accepted the position but had no way to escape.

Gregory was meant to preside the Council of Constantinople in 381 AD. However, his opponents pointed out that he was already a bishop in Sasima, and thus could not preside over the council. Gregory resigned from his position as Archbishop of Constantinople and retired to a monastery in Nazianzus, where he spent the rest of his life writing theological works and sermons.

(council-of-constantinople)=
The Council of Constantinople in 381 AD was a significant event in the history of Christianity. It was convened by Emperor Theodosius I to address ongoing theological disputes, particularly the Arian controversy. The council reaffirmed the [Nicene Creed](#nicaea) and expanded it to include a more detailed description of the Holy Spirit's divinity. It also condemned various heresies, including Arianism, Apollinarianism, and Macedonianism.
