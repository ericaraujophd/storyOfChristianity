(john-chrysostom)=
John Chrysostom, whose name means "Golden Mouth" in Greek, was one of the most renowned early Church Fathers and preachers. Born around 347 AD in Antioch, he became famous for his eloquent sermons and writings that emphasized moral reform and social justice. He was in essence a monk turned priest turned bishop.

Anthusa, John's mother, was a devout Christian who played a significant role in his early religious education. She ensured that he received a solid foundation in Christian teachings and values. She was glad to hear that John wanted to be baptized when he was about 20 years old. She wasn't so joyful when he decided to become a monk shortly after that and made him promise to wait until after her death.

To solve the domestic problem, John made his house into a monastery, and lived there as a monk with a few friends. They practiced extreme asceticism, including fasting and vigils. After his mother's death, John went to the Syrian mountais to learn the disciplines of the monastic life. He spent four years learning, and then two more years in seclusion, living in a cave.

After his time in the desert, John returned to Antioch and was ordained a deacon and then a presbyter short after. He quickly gained a reputation for his powerful preaching and deep commitment to social justice. His sermons often addressed issues such as poverty, corruption, and the moral decay of society.

In 397 AD the bishopric of Constantinople became vacant, and John was chosen to fill the position. To not cause a riot in Antioch, he was kidnapped and taken to Constantinople, where he was consacrated in 398 AD. As bishop, he continued his preaching and social reform efforts, often clashing with the wealthy and powerful elite of the city. He was particularly critical of the excesses of the imperial court and the moral laxity of the clergy.

Among his reform efforts, John ordered:

- that the "spiritual sisters" move out of the priests' homes (a scandal at the time)
- that priests live an austere life
- that church finances be managed more transparently
- that the luxurious items adorning the churches be sold to help the poor
- that the clergy would open the doors at all hours to permit that workers and the poor to enter and receive assistance

His clashes with the imperial family, particularly Empress Eudoxia, led to his eventual exile in 403 AD. He was accused of various charges, including insubordination and heresy, and was forced to leave Constantinople. He died in exile in 407 AD.

Eudoxia felt like Chrysostom's preachings fitted her like a glove. She didn't like the eyes of the people turning to her as the example of luxury and excess that Chrysostom condemned. She then plotted to get rid of him raising false accusations against him.

John decided to leave the city in peace, to avoid a civil war. On the day of his departure, a massive earthquake struck Constantinople, which many saw as a divine sign of displeasure at John's exile. Despite this, John was sent away. Fearing the wrath of God, Eudoxia later called him back.

Later, Chrysostom was exiled again to a remote location in the Cucusus. This time, the violence was greater, and many of his followers were tortured and exiled too.

The Bishop of Rome, Innocent I, protested strongly against the treatment of Chrysostom, but to no avail. John Chrysostom died in exile in 407 AD after being expelled from Cucusus.
