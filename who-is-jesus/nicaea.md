Until 300 A.D. the main theological debates revolved around the Canon of Scripture and the defense of the faith against heresies like Gnosticism. But that would change in the 4th century. With the conversion of Constantine and the legalization of Christianity, the church faced new challenges. Arianism emerged as a significant theological controversy, questioning the nature of Christ and his relationship to God the Father.

:::{figure} figures/trifacial-trinity-1770-anonymous-cusco-school.jpg
Trifacial Trinity, 1770, Anonymous, Cusco School
:::

This doctrine found considerable support, even among some bishops and emperors. Christianity, first seen as a unifying religion to the Roman Empire, now faced internal divisions. The need to address these theological disputes led to the convening of ecumenical councils, starting with the Council of Nicaea in 325 A.D.

For the church of the 4th century, all these things should be tied together:

- **Canon:** what texts are authoritative?
- **Creed:** what do we believe?
- **Preaching:** what do we teach?
- **Councils:** what do we agree upon?
- **Eucharist:** how do we celebrate?

After defining the canon and formulating creeds, the church now had to address the theological controversies that arose within its own ranks.

## The Trinitarian Controversy (4th century)

Before the Council of Nicaea, various views about the nature of Christ and his relationship to God the Father circulated within the Christian community. One of the most prominent was [Arianism](#arianism), which argued that Jesus was a created being and not co-eternal with the Father.

Tertullian was the first to use the term "Trinity" to describe the relationship between the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. He emphasized that while there are three distinct persons, they share one substance (one *substantia* consisting in three *personae*).

(modalism)=
Some followers of Sabellius proposed the Modalism, which suggested that God is one person who appears in different modes or forms (Father, Son, Holy Spirit) rather than being three distinct persons. This view was also known as Modalistic Monarchianism.

(adoptionism)=
A second type of Monarchianism, Dynamic Monarchianism, argued that Jesus was not divine by nature but became divine through adoption by God. This view is often associated with the teachings of Paul of Samosata. It is also known as Adoptionism.

Due to the popularity of discussions about the Trinity and the nature of Christ, the Council of Nicaea emerged in 325 A.D.

It is important to note that the concept of Monarchianism emerges as a reaction to the heresies of the time.

## The Council of Nicaea (325 A.D.)

(nicaea)=
The Council of Nicaea, held in 325 A.D., was the first ecumenical council of the Christian Church. It was convened by Emperor Constantine I in the city of Nicaea (modern-day İznik, Turkey) to address the Arian controversy and to establish a unified Christian doctrine. The council brought together bishops from across the Roman Empire to discuss and resolve theological disputes that were threatening the unity of the church.

:::{figure} figures/nicaea-synod-holy-fathers-modified-theologic-og-1200.jpg
The First Council of Nicaea. Icon, 14th century.
:::

Most of the bishops who attended the Council of Nicaea were from the East. Only 6 of them came from the West. Still, there was a global representation in the room.

The low attendance from the West can be attributed to the fact that the Western Church was less centralized and faced more immediate threats from barbarian invasions, which made travel and participation more difficult.

Some of the important figures present at this council include [Athanasius](#athanasius), who attended but did not vote.

**Key Figures at the Council**

| Name | Origin | Role at the Council |
|------|--------|---------------------|
| **Constantine I** | Nicomedia (Roman Empire) | Emperor who convened the council; presided over sessions but did not vote; sought unity in the church |
| **Hosius of Cordova** | Cordova, Spain | Bishop who likely presided over theological discussions; advisor to Constantine; supporter of orthodoxy |
| **Alexander of Alexandria** | Alexandria, Egypt | Patriarch of Alexandria; primary opponent of Arianism; leader of the orthodox position |
| **Athanasius** | Alexandria, Egypt | Deacon and secretary to Alexander; defender of orthodoxy; later became Patriarch of Alexandria (did not vote at the council) |
| **Arius** | Libya/Alexandria, Egypt | Presbyter of Alexandria; proponent of Arianism (the view that the Son was created and subordinate to the Father) |
| **Eusebius of Nicomedia** | Nicomedia (modern Turkey) | Bishop; supporter of Arius; influential in the imperial court |
| **Eusebius of Caesarea** | Caesarea, Palestine | Bishop and church historian; initially sympathetic to Arius; proposed a creed that was modified |
| **Nicholas of Myra** | Myra, Lycia (modern Turkey) | Bishop; strong opponent of Arianism (later became St. Nicholas) |
| **Marcellus of Ancyra** | Ancyra (modern Ankara, Turkey) | Bishop; opponent of Arianism |
| **Eustathius of Antioch** | Antioch, Syria | Patriarch of Antioch; strong defender of Nicene orthodoxy |
| **Paphnutius of Thebes** | Upper Egypt | Bishop and confessor; bore marks of persecution; opposed mandatory clerical celibacy |
| **Spyridon of Trimythous** | Cyprus | Bishop; defender of orthodoxy; known for his simplicity and miracles |

The discussion revolved around Arius arguments that there was a time when the Son was not (a created being). For him, the Son of God was created, and then adopted into divinity. Arius was concerned to protect God's attributes (one, eternal, unchangeable, transcendent), so he draws a line in the Greek hierarchy of being and places Son on the creature side of line.

The Council will respond to that saying that the Son is *homoousious* (of the same substance) with the Father. 

The neoplatonic equation that Arius used to understand the nature of Jesus was the idea of the structure of **One -> Nous -> Soul -> World**. That was a common way of thinking in the Hellenistic worldview. All things emanate from the **One** and all things will return to the **One**. The **Nous** and **Soul** emanate from the **One**.  The **Soul** generates the world. Things are good insofar as they exist. Evil is the absence of good.

The gospel narrative of the time would be that the Bible is about salvation. That is a starting point (in contrast with Greek philosophy).

The fact is that, even after the Council of Nicaea had decided the way to understand the nature of Christ, debates continued. Between 325 and 381 A.D., the world was thrown in a discussion about *homoousious* and *homoiousios*. Reports tell us that even the laypeople were debating these terms in the marketplaces.

According to Gregory of Nyssa,

> Every part of the city is filled with such talk: the alleys, the crossroads, the squares, the avenues. It comes from those who sell clothes, money changers, grocers. If you ask a money changer what the exchange rate is, he will reply with a dissertation on the begotten and the unbegotten. If you enquire about the quality and the price of bread, the baker will reply: ‘The Father is greatest and the Son is subject to him.’ When you ask at the baths whether the water is ready, the manager will declare that the Son came forth from nothing. I do not know what name to give to this evil, whether frenzy or madness... [*On the Divinity of the Son and the Holy Spirit*, V]

[Athanasius](#athanasius) spent a great deal of his life defending the Nicene Creed. Later he would receive the reinforcement of the Cappadocian Fathers: [Basil the Great](#basil), [Gregory of Nazianzus](#gregory_of_nazianzus), and [Gregory of Nyssa](#gregory_of_nyssa). This new party will help to form the idea that God is 3 hypostases (distinctiveness of consciousness of each person of the Trinity. Another term for "person") in one *ousia* (essence or substance).

One of his main arguments was that the Word, who is God, called creation into existence. Disobedience causes humanity to fall back toward non-existence. He will also present the divine dilemma:

- God can’t let His creation return to non-existence **AND**
- God can’t break His word that sin leads to death.

Therefore, the same Word that called us into existence in the beginning attaches himself to our humanity in order to call humanity back into existence. The Incarnate Word defeats death, bringing life to Adam and all who believe. The participation in the Word's humanity restores the image of God in us.

> The Son of God became the Son of Man so that children of men could become children of God.

(pneumatomachians)=
Also, during this period, the Macedonians denied the divinity of the Holy Spirit. They were also known as pneumatomachians (fighters against the Spirit).

That is to say that there was a lot going on in the 4th century regarding the understanding of the Trinity. And other councils will be necessary to clarify these matters, such as the [Council of Constantinople](#constantinople) in 381 A.D.

## Eusebius of Nicomedia and the Aftermath

It suffices to say that Eusebius of Nicomedia would soon revert the results of Nicaea mainly due to his influence with Constantine. He would convince the emperor that his Arian case was valid, and that the emperor was too harsh on the Arians. Constantine would exile [Athanasius](#athanasius), the new champion of the Nicene cause and bishop of Alexandria, and other Nicene supporters. Eusebius of Nicomedia would later baptize Constantine on his deathbed.

After Constantine's death, his three sons, Constantius II, Constantine II, and Constans, would rule different parts of the empire. At first, they maintained a policy of religious tolerance, but over time, Constantius II, who ruled the Eastern part of the empire, became a strong supporter of Arianism. He actively promoted Arian bishops and suppressed Nicene Christianity.

After the death of Constantine II, the West was united under Constans, who was a supporter of Nicene Christianity. However, after Constans' death, Constantius II became the sole ruler of the entire Roman Empire. Under his reign, Arianism gained significant influence, and Nicene Christians faced persecution.

According to Jerome, "the entire world woke from a deep slumber and discovered that it had become Arian." Even Liberius, bishop of Rome, signed the Arian confession of faith in 357 A.D. to avoid exile.

## Julian the Apostate

(julian)=
The reign of Constantius II ended in 361 A.D., and he was succeeded by his cousin Julian, known as Julian the Apostate. Julian was a pagan who sought to restore traditional Roman religious practices and reduce the influence of Christianity in the empire. He allowed Nicene Christians to return from exile and restored their properties, but he also promoted paganism and sought to undermine Christianity.

In short, Constantius II declared Julian, his cousin, as his successor and gave him control over Gaul. Julian spent a great deal of his life in Athens studying pagan philosophy and religion. He even met [Basil of Caesarea](#basil) during his time there. When he started his government in Gaul, he quickly proved to be a capable military leader by defeating the Germanic tribes that were threatening the region. His army proclaimed him emperor in 360 A.D., leading to a civil war with Constantius II. Constantius II died in 361 A.D. during the conflict, and Julian became the sole ruler of the Roman Empire.

Julian attempted to revive paganism and reduce the influence of Christianity, which he saw as a threat to traditional Roman values. Julian's policies included restoring pagan temples, promoting pagan festivals, and attempting to reform the pagan priesthood.

Julian's reign was short-lived, as he died in 363 A.D. during a military campaign against the Sassanian Empire. After his death, Jovian, a Christian, became emperor and restored Nicene Christianity as the dominant faith of the empire.

Legend claims that his last words were: "Thou hast conquered, Galilean," referring to Jesus Christ.
