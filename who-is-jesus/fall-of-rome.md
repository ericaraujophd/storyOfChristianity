---
title: The Fall of Rome (313-476 AD)
subtitle: The End of the Ancient Church Period
---

When Augustine died, the Vandals were already taking over the city of Hippo. The Western Roman Empire was in its final stages, and the fall of Rome was imminent. In 410 AD, the Visigoths, led by Alaric, sacked Rome, marking a significant event in the decline of the Western Roman Empire. This event shocked the Christian world, as Rome had been the center of the empire and a symbol of stability.

An earlier battle that foreshadowed the fall of Rome was the Battle of Adrianople in 378 AD, where the Goths defeated the Roman army and killed Emperor Valens. This battle exposed the vulnerabilities of the Roman military and highlighted the growing power of barbarian groups within the empire.

The Germanic tribes, including the Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Vandals, and Franks, played a crucial role in the fall of the Western Roman Empire. These tribes were initially settled within the empire as foederati (allied peoples) but eventually turned against Rome due to mistreatment and broken promises.

The final blow to the Western Roman Empire came in 476 AD when the Germanic chieftain Odoacer deposed the last Roman emperor, Romulus Augustulus. This event is traditionally marked as the end of the Western Roman Empire and the beginning of the Middle Ages in Europe.

The imperial church would still last for another thousand years in the East, known as the Byzantine Empire, with its capital in Constantinople. The Eastern Roman Empire preserved much of the Roman legal and cultural heritage and played a significant role in the history of Christianity.

As for the West, the church would keep the heritage of the order and civilization until the empire would be restored under Charlemagne in 800 AD. From that moment onward, the church would crown emperors, and the relationship between church and state would be a defining feature of medieval Europe.
