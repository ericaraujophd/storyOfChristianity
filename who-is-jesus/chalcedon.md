---
title: The Council of Chalcedon (451 A.D.)
subtitle: Defining the Two Natures of Christ

(chalcedon)=
The Council of Chalcedon, held in 451 A.D., was the fourth ecumenical council of the Christian Church. It was convened in the city of Chalcedon (modern-day Kadıköy, Turkey) to address ongoing Christological controversies, particularly those related to the nature of Jesus Christ. The council aimed to clarify and define the orthodox understanding of Christ's divinity and humanity in response to various heretical teachings.

Hellenistic philosophy pictures a cosmic intermediary who is neither God nor man. The church gradually worked toward an articulation of Christ as fully God and fully man in a concrete individual person. God in the flesh is personal, relational, and accommodating, none of which compromise God’s eternal, unchangeable, transcendent attributes.

Amongst the various options to interpret the person of Jesus, we can think of:

- Not human?
- 1 person w/ 1 divine soul?
- 1 person w/ 1 divine spirit?
- 2 persons w/ 2 natures?
- 1 person w/ 2 natures?
- 1 person w/ 1 nature?
- 1 person w/ 1 will?

The primary issue at the Council of Chalcedon then was the debate over how to understand the relationship between Jesus' divine and human natures. Several options had emerged before this council:

| Position | Proponent | Persons | Natures | Key Claim | Status |
|----------|-----------|---------|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Apollinarianism** | Apollinaris of Laodicea | 1 | 1 nature (divine spirit/soul in human body) | Jesus had a human body but the divine Logos replaced his rational soul, denying complete humanity  | Heresy |
| **Cyrillianism** | Cyril of Alexandria | 1 | 2 natures (divine & human) | The Logos is the center of Jesus' personhood, uniting both natures without confusion or separation | Orthodox (became Chalcedonian position) |
| **Nestorianism** | Nestorius of Constantinople | 2 | 2 natures | Jesus was two separate persons—one divine and one human—analogous to a man and wife in marriage | Heresy (condemned at Ephesus 431) |
| **Monophysitism** | Eutyches of Alexandria | 1 | 1 nature (fusion of divine & human) | Jesus had one fused nature (*tertium quid*), where the human was absorbed by the divine, denying full humanity | Heresy (condemned at Chalcedon 451) |
| **Tome of Leo** | Pope Leo I (the Great) | 1 | 2 natures (divine & human) | Two distinct natures united without confusion, change, division, or separation | Orthodox (became foundational at Chalcedon) |

The council of calcedon (451) then decided that:

1. Jesus is one person.
2. Jesus has two natures. He is perfect man and perfect God (contra Apollinaris).
3. The two natures are inseparable, indivisible (contra Nestorius).
4. The two natures are unconfused, unchanged (contra Eutyches).
5. Jesus is “theotokos, according to the flesh	(i.e. Mary didn't give birth to the divine nature).

The Chalcedonian definition was accepted by both East and West, but some churches rejected it, especially the monophysites.

(monophysitism)=
According to the Monophysites, Jesus has only one nature (divine). The human nature is absorbed by the divine nature. This idea was later condemned by the Council of Constantinople II in 553 A.D.

(monothelitism)=
The Monothelites, on the other hand, believed that Jesus has two natures but only one will (the divine). The human will is absorbed by the divine will.

This was an attempt to  compromise between Eutychians and Chalcedon. Some important figures, such as the popes, emperors, and patriarchs promoted this position in an attempt to unite church in face of Islam.

However, this position was also condemned as heretical at the Third Council of Constantinople in 680-681 A.D. In this council, it was affirmed that Jesus had a full human will, with his human will freely assenting to suffering.

(schism)=
The Christological controversies were not just theological debates but had significant political and social implications. Different regions and churches aligned themselves with different Christological positions, leading to schisms and the formation of separate churches. This is the moment when real schisms start to happen in the church.

### The Monophysite Churches

The Monophysite churches, which rejected the Chalcedonian definition, include the Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria, the Syriac Orthodox Church, the Armenian Apostolic Church, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. These churches continue to uphold the belief in the single (divine) nature of Christ.
